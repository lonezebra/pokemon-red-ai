What Is This, Exactly?

We're teaching a computer to play Pokemon Red with no walkthrough and no script telling it what to do. It starts knowing nothing — not what a door is, not what a trainer is, not that losing a battle is bad. It learns the way you'd train a very persistent, very fast toddler: try things thousands of times, get small rewards for progress and penalties for mistakes, and slowly figure out what works.

The one rule the project holds itself to: scripts are only allowed as scaffolding. It's fine to walk the character somewhere once by hand to learn what the game's memory looks like there, or to save a checkpoint so training doesn't replay the intro every time — but the actual playing has to be learned. Rather than pointing one giant AI at the whole game, it's broken into bite-sized challenges — leave the house, cross a route, win a battle — each one trained until mastered, then chained together. The near-term target is Brock, the first Gym Leader. The long-term dream, if the approach keeps scaling: the Elite Four.

The Route 3 Story

Route 3 was "solved" once already — 463 rounds, 184,836 episodes, 95.5% success — before anyone noticed the goal tile it was solving for wasn't real. It was the deepest point an automated map survey could reach, not an actual exit. The survey's own method made it structurally blind to what was past that point.

The bug

Gen 1 trainers battle exactly once. A survey that restores to a pre-battle snapshot before testing each direction can never see what opens up after a trainer is genuinely defeated — so the automated exploration hit a wall of undefeated trainers and concluded nothing lay beyond it. 20+ automated retests all agreed. All of them were wrong.

The find

Not something automation could have caught: the fix came from a human who actually knew the map — "you can get to Mt. Moon that way by beating more of them" — followed by a save state and an exact button sequence played back manually to prove the path was real: beat the trainer, back up a tile, go around.

The fix

A real flood-fill from the post-victory position, merged with the old survey's data, took the known map from 125 tiles / 384 edges / 4 exits to 435 tiles / 1,430 edges / 9 exits — 5 of them genuine doors into Mt. Moon. Training was retargeted at the real goal and restarted from scratch.

The re-surveyed Route 3, stitched from the emulator's own screen: the corridor the old survey knew stops less than halfway across. Everything east of it — including the Mt. Moon entrance — only became visible once the survey learned to fight its way through.

Milestones Along the Way

The route map above is the headline, but most of the project's life is spent on the unglamorous steps that make each route trainable.

learned
Leaving the bedroom & reaching Professor Oak — the project's first two learned behaviors, proof the whole reward-and-repetition loop works before pointing it at anything hard.
learned
Three battle brains — separate neural-network policies for rival battles, wild encounters, and trainer fights. The navigation agents hand off to them mid-walk whenever a fight starts, then pick the walk back up.
learned
Catching Pokemon — the wild-battle brain got a throw-a-ball action. Took five retrains to get right: the first attempts never caught anything because a tracking bug made the game think everything was already caught — and, memorably, because every save state up to then carried zero Poke Balls.
scaffold
The Route 3 party grind — 143 scripted battles raising the team from Lv13 to Lv20, because the entry-level party was losing up to 72% of its HP per fight and couldn't survive what exploration would throw at it.
scaffold
Map surveys — automated crawls that walk every reachable tile of a route and record exactly which steps work, producing the maps the reward signals are built from. Route 3's needed two tries — see the story above.
infra
The training harness itself — 14 parallel emulators with self-balancing workloads, resume-from-checkpoint after any interruption, live progress reports with honest ETAs, and a "finish by 3 PM so I can close the laptop and get my kid" deadline mode.

AUG 3, 4:11 PM – 8:42 PM PDT

Round 16 appears to freeze at 42%, then breaks loose

Eight consecutive 15-minute status checks all read the same: 166/400 episodes claimed, no forward movement, for two full hours. ps aux showed all 14 workers at ~100% CPU with accumulated CPU time tracking wall-clock elapsed almost exactly — genuinely computing, not deadlocked. A timed replay of the exact conditions (same map, same ~0.4 epsilon, the real trainer-battle model) clocked worst-case, max-length episodes at ~25 seconds each: 20–30× faster than the observed rate implied, ruling out "these are just long episodes" as the explanation on its own. Leading theory: sustained multi-hour full-core load on a laptop is a classic thermal-throttling trigger — clock speed drops while usage still reads ~100%, indistinguishable from healthy progress unless you go looking. The round then recovered on its own, climbing steadily from 166→345/400 (86%) by the last check before finishing.

Computational Horsepower

Everything here runs on one machine, trained in the open, in between the owner's actual life.

Hardware
Apple M-series, 18 cores
6 high-performance “super” cores + 12 standard performance cores — no hyperthreading, so every pinned core is a full physical core.
Allocation
14 cores pinned
4 held back so the machine stays usable for everything else while training runs for days at a stretch.
Scheduling
QoS-biased, not hard-pinned
macOS has no CPU affinity API. Workers self-tag QOS_CLASS_UTILITY to steer off the super-core tier under contention, switchable between full-priority and low-priority-yielding runs.
Work distribution
Self-balancing claims
Workers pull from a shared episode budget instead of a fixed split, so faster cores simply do more — no idle tail waiting on the slowest worker.

Keeping the compute honest

A CPU-pegged process isn't always a healthy one — round 16's apparent two-hour freeze (see Recent Checkpoints above) turned out to most likely be thermal throttling, not a hang. Worth remembering on any multi-day, full-core run: "still computing" and "computing at full speed" read identically in ps.
